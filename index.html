Fake news vs. reality: a guide to fact-checking

Every day we see hundreds of headlines, posts and videos. Some are true, some are wrong, and some are made to fool us. This guide shows how to tell the difference in five minutes or less.

Learn the 5 steps Take the quiz

Why does it matter?

False information is not just a harmless mistake on the internet.

It spreads fast

A shocking post gets shared before anyone checks it. Social networks reward posts that make people react, not posts that are accurate.

It affects decisions

People use news to decide what to buy, whom to trust, how to treat their health and how to vote. Wrong information leads to wrong choices.

Anyone can be fooled

Being smart is not enough. Fake news is designed to look convincing and to match what we already believe or fear.

Three words that are often mixed up

TermMeaningExample
MisinformationFalse information shared by someone who believes it is true.A student reposts an old photo of a flood, thinking it shows today's event.
DisinformationFalse information created and shared on purpose to mislead or to make money.A website invents a story about a "miracle cure" to sell a product.
MalinformationReal information used to harm someone, for example private details made public.Someone leaks a private message to humiliate a person.

7 types of misleading content

Researcher Claire Wardle describes seven forms of information disorder. Not all of them are completely "fake": often a true detail is placed in a false frame.

1

Satire or parody

A joke with no intention to harm, but readers may take it seriously if they miss the joke.

2

Misleading content

A real fact or quote is used to frame an issue in a distorted way.

3

Imposter content

A fake source pretends to be a real person, newspaper or organization.

4

Fabricated content

Completely false: invented text, a made-up story, a fake document.

5

False connection

The headline or picture does not match what the article actually says (clickbait).

6

False context

A real photo, video or quote is shared with wrong information about when, where or why it happened.

7

Manipulated content

A real image, video or audio recording is edited or generated to deceive.

Real cases and their consequences

Fake news is not only an online problem. These are well-known cases where false information caused real damage.

2016 · USA · Conspiracy theory

“Pizzagate”

The fake: a story spread on social media claiming that a Washington pizzeria hid a criminal ring.
Consequence: a man who believed it came to the restaurant with a rifle and fired shots inside. Nobody was hurt, but he was sent to prison, and the business received threats for a long time.

2018 · India · Messenger rumors

Child-kidnapper rumors in WhatsApp

The fake: forwarded messages and videos warned about “child kidnappers” in villages.
Consequence: angry crowds attacked strangers, and according to media reports about two dozen innocent people were killed. WhatsApp then limited message forwarding.

2013 · USA · Hacked account

Fake tweet about the White House

The fake: hackers posted from the Associated Press account that there were explosions at the White House and the president was injured.
Consequence: within minutes the US stock market fell sharply, by roughly 150 points on the Dow index, and recovered after the news was denied.

2020 · Iran · Health rumor

“Alcohol cures coronavirus”

The fake: a rumor that strong alcohol protects against or cures COVID-19.
Consequence: people drank cheap illegal alcohol containing methanol. Hundreds died and thousands were poisoned.

2020 · UK and Europe · Conspiracy theory

5G towers and the virus

The fake: a claim that 5G networks spread or cause COVID-19.
Consequence: dozens of mobile phone masts were set on fire, and telecom engineers were threatened and attacked.

1998–2010 · Medicine · Fraudulent study

Vaccines and autism

The fake: a published study claimed a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. It was later found to be fraudulent, and the journal retracted it in 2010.
Consequence: many parents refused vaccination, and measles outbreaks returned in several countries.

What these cases have in common: the story was emotional, spread fast and was shared by people who wanted to help or warn others. Checking it first would have taken minutes.

The 5-step fact-check

Use these steps whenever a story looks surprising, emotional or too perfect. They are based on well-known media literacy methods such as SIFT and the CRAAP test.

  1. Stop and notice your reaction

    Are you angry, scared or thrilled? Strong emotion is a warning sign. Do not share or comment yet. Ask yourself: "Do I know this source, and is this claim really proven?"

  2. Check the source

    Who published it? Open the "About" page, look for a real author, contact details and a history of other articles. Check the web address carefully: fake sites often copy real names with small changes.

  3. Read beyond the headline

    Headlines are written to attract clicks. Read the full text: does it give evidence, named experts, documents or data? Or does it only say "they say" and "insiders claim"?

  4. Find other reports

    Search for the main claim on your own. If it is real and important, several independent and reliable outlets will report it. If only one unknown website has it, be careful.

  5. Check the date, photo and video

    Old stories get reposted as new. Look at the publication date. Use reverse image search to find where a picture appeared first. Watch for signs of editing or AI generation.

Red flags

One red flag does not prove a story is fake, but several together mean you should check carefully.

  • A shocking or very emotional headline in CAPITAL LETTERS or with many exclamation marks!!!
  • No author, no date, no links to sources.
  • "Share before they delete it!" or other pressure to act quickly.
  • Spelling mistakes and strange design on a site that claims to be a big news outlet.
  • The story only confirms what you already want to believe.
  • A screenshot instead of a link: it is easy to fake and hard to verify.
  • No other reliable media report the same "sensational" fact.

Example: checking a viral post

An imaginary case to show the 5 steps in action.

Post: "SHOCKING! Scientists confirm that students who sleep only 3 hours get better grades! Share before it's removed!"

Step 1 – Stop

The post uses capital letters and pressure ("before it's removed"). Red flags already.

Step 2 – Source

No link, no name of the "scientists", no author of the post.

Step 3 – Read

There is no article behind the headline, only a claim.

Step 4 – Other reports

Searching finds no study. Health organizations say the opposite: students need regular, sufficient sleep to learn well.

Verdict: not supported by evidence. Do not share.

About this project

Project type

Social project. Its aim is to change a problem situation for the better: students learn to recognize false information and stop it from spreading.

Product

An educational website with a guide, a self-test and a checklist that anyone can use for free.

Target audience

University and school students who get most of their news from social networks and messengers.

Conclusion

We cannot avoid fake news completely, but we can slow it down. Before you share: stop, check the source, read, compare and verify the picture.
